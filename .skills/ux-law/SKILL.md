## Review an interaction

1. Identify the user, their goal, task context, and interface state. Inspect the supplied screen, flow, prototype, research, or product evidence before judging it.
2. Explain the observed problem in plain terms. Separate what is directly visible or measured from the explanation you infer.
3. Select only laws or methods that illuminate the specific problem. For each, say what mechanism it suggests and why that mechanism may apply here.
4. Critique the fit: identify assumptions, counterexamples, tradeoffs, and evidence that would weaken the interpretation. A principle is a hypothesis source, not proof of a design rule.
5. Suggest a concrete change only when it follows from the surviving evidence. State a test that could show whether the change helped.

## Facilitate a deck-based exercise

When the user asks to use the 54-card deck for ideation or a workshop, preserve the distinction between principle cards and UX-method cards. First define the product question and user outcome; select relevant cards from the available deck; use them to generate alternatives; then converge by checking evidence, feasibility, accessibility, and the user's goal. Do not claim to have selected or reviewed cards that were not supplied or accessible.

## Report findings

For each design finding, provide:

- **Observation:** the interaction or evidence.
- **Law or method:** the relevant item and its role in the analysis.
- **Hypothesis:** the predicted user effect and a condition that could falsify it.
- **Change:** the smallest useful design adjustment, if supported.
- **Validation:** the task, measure, or evidence needed to decide whether the change helped.

A UX law does not override accessibility requirements, user research, domain constraints, or direct evidence from users. If no listed principle explains the issue, review the interaction on its own terms.
