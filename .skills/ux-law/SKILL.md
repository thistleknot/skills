# UX Law Review

Use UX principles as prompts for investigation, not as proof that a design is good or bad. The current Laws of UX catalog is maintained at [lawsofux.com/laws](https://lawsofux.com/laws/); consult it when the requested principle or its wording is unclear. Do not rely on the old note's unverified "54-card deck" count.

## Review the actual interaction

1. Identify the user, their goal, the task context, and the part of the interface being reviewed. Inspect the supplied screen, flow, prototype, or product evidence before judging it.
2. Identify only the principles that explain a concrete observed friction or design decision. Summarize each principle in plain language and link it to the relevant moment in the interaction.
3. Separate observation from inference. State what the interface does, what user effect you predict, and what evidence would disconfirm that prediction.
4. Recommend a specific change only when it addresses the observed issue. Note tradeoffs and plausible counterexamples, including cases where following a principle would make another task worse.
5. Name a validation method that can test the claim, such as a task-based usability check, an accessibility check, or a measured comparison. State the task and success signal; do not invent results.

## Report findings

For each finding, provide:

- **Observation:** the visible interaction or evidence.
- **Principle:** the relevant UX law or heuristic and why it applies here.
- **Hypothesis:** the expected user effect and a condition that could falsify it.
- **Change:** the smallest useful design adjustment, if supported.
- **Validation:** the task, measure, or evidence needed to decide whether the change helped.

Keep recommendations grounded in the user's goal. A principle does not override accessibility requirements, user research, domain constraints, or direct evidence from the product's users. If no listed law explains the issue, say so and review the interaction on its own terms.
